## Part 1 — The animal fact and why it happens

| # | Claim as narrated | Source(s) | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Dolphins can sleep with one brain hemisphere in deep slow-wave sleep while the other is awake, then the halves swap ("unihemispheric slow-wave sleep"). | Mukhametov et al. 1977 (*Brain Research*); Lyamin et al. 2008 (*Neurosci. Biobehav. Rev.*) | ✅ |
| 2 | Humans normally sleep with both hemispheres slowing down together. | Lyamin et al. 2008; Rattenborg et al. 2000 | ✅ |
| 3 | In dolphins nearly all optic fibers cross to the opposite hemisphere, so the open eye is usually the one wired to the awake half. When the left half sleeps, it's often the **left** eye that stays open. | Rattenborg et al. 2000; Lyamin et al. 2008 | ✅ The script says "often" because eye state doesn't match brain state 100% of the time. |
| 4 | Dolphins are voluntary breathers: they breathe at the surface through the blowhole, and breathing isn't automatic the way it is for us. Keeping half the brain awake lets them keep surfacing. | Lyamin et al. 2008; NOAA Fisheries (bottlenose dolphin) | ⚠️ Narrated as "the leading idea", because it's a hypothesis. |
| 5 | Half-awake may also help with predator vigilance, staying with the group and staying warm. Researchers still debate how much each reason matters. | Lyamin et al. 2008; Rattenborg et al. 2000 | ⚠️ Narrated as debated. |
| 6 | Some researchers reported newborn calves and mothers barely rest for weeks after birth; others dispute it. | Lyamin et al. 2005 (*Nature*) vs Gnone et al. 2006 (*Nature*) | ⚠️ Both sides cited and the dispute is said out loud. |
| 7 | Fur seals sleep with both halves at once on land, but mostly one half at a time in the water. | Lyamin et al. 2018 (*Current Biology*) | ✅ |
| 8 | Sperm whales take short upright naps near the surface, about 7% of their time. | Miller et al. 2008 (*Current Biology*) | ✅ About 7% of recorded time, in tagged whales. |
| 9 | Two US Navy dolphins were trained to keep detecting targets with echolocation; one kept it up for 15 days straight without slipping. | Branstetter et al. 2012 (*PLoS ONE*) | ✅ The title of the paper states "15 days without interruption or cognitive impairment". |
| 10 | Ducks at the edge of a sleeping group keep more of their brain awake, with the open eye facing outward toward danger. | Rattenborg et al. 1999 (*Nature*, mallards) | ✅ |
| 11 | Great frigatebirds can sleep in flight over the ocean for days, about 40 minutes a day in the air, compared with over 12 hours a day on land. | Rattenborg et al. 2016 (*Nature Communications*) | ✅ About 0.7 h/day in flight (≈42 min); flights lasted up to about 10 days. |

## Part 2 — What if YOU had it

| # | Claim as narrated | Source(s) | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| 12 | First night in a new place: part of the **left** hemisphere stays more alert during deep sleep and reacts faster to strange sounds (the "first-night effect"), one reason first nights away are often bad sleep. | Tamaki et al. 2016 (*Current Biology*) | ✅ "A built-in night watch" is the authors' own interpretation. |
| 13 | In most right-handed people, language lives mainly in the left hemisphere. | Knecht et al. 2000 (*Brain*) | ✅ No exact percentage is narrated. |
| 14 | So with the left half asleep, "you might struggle to talk". | 🧮 extrapolation from #13 | 🧮 Hypothetical, worded "might". |
| 15 | Each hemisphere controls the opposite side of the body (left half asleep → clumsy right hand). | NINDS (NIH) *Brain Basics* | ✅ The clumsy hand is 🧮 reasoning from this. |
| 16 | About 9 in 10 people are right-handed. | Papadatou-Pastou et al. 2020 (*Psychological Bulletin*): ~10.6% left-handed | ✅ |
| 17 | About half the nerve fibers from each human eye cross at the optic chiasm, so each eye talks to both halves. | NCBI StatPearls, *Optic Chiasm* | ✅ |
| 18 | Dolphins still sleep; each half still gets its rest, just in shifts. | Lyamin et al. 2008 | ✅ |
| 19 | Adults need at least 7 hours of sleep a night. | CDC, *About Sleep* | ✅ |

## Things I deliberately left out (myths or too uncertain)

- **"Dolphins never sleep" / "dolphins don't dream":** REM sleep in cetaceans is absent or unclear and still debated (Lyamin 2008), so the episode doesn't mention dreaming.
- **"Sleep half the time with each half":** exact hours per hemisphere vary by species and study, so no number is narrated.
- **Exact breath-hold times** for bottlenose dolphins: sources vary, so none are given.
